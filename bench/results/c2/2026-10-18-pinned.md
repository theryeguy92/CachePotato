# Core pinning at 2 GB: 2.1× fewer reads, and the first policy win in this phase

**Date:** 2026-10-18 · **Branch:** `feat/qwen3-30b-a3b` · **Status: measured, recommended, opt-in.**

The low-budget arms showed the hot tier being evicted along with the experts: at 2 GB the core hit
rate was 10.77% because 1.60 GB of core in a 2 GB budget leaves ~0.42 GB for 6,144 expert regions,
and LRU has no notion that the attention weights will be wanted again in the same token. Pinning the
435 core regions is the cheapest fix — and it is something only an explicit pager can do, since the
OS page cache cannot protect a chosen subset of a file against its own LRU.

## What changed

`CP_PIN_CORE=1` marks every non-expert region un-evictable. Pinned regions still **count against the
budget** — pinning is a policy about *who* gets evicted, not a way to get memory for free — and if
the budget is below the pinned floor the pager says so once and keeps running rather than silently
exceeding the cap. Reports now print the pinned floor and the evictable remainder:

```
  pinned    : 1560.3 MB never evicted (CP_PIN_CORE) — evictable capacity 508.6 MB for experts
```

Bit-identity is preserved: `verify_qwen3_paging.py` now also runs a tight-budget pinned pass and
requires it to be byte-identical to the unpaged run. It is.

## The measurement (cold start, interleaved, 2 reps, 32 tokens; deterministic counters identical)

| budget | policy | regions/tok | MB/tok | evict/tok | expert hit | **core hit** | phys MB/tok | ms/tok | tok/s |
|---|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 2 GB | global LRU | 818.9 | 3589.8 | 815.8 | 4.68% | **10.77%** | 3509.5 ± 34.4 | 11738 | 0.085 |
| 2 GB | **core pinned** | **384.0** | 1925.2 | 386.1 | 4.68% | **97.30%** | 2110.1 ± 4.0 | 10008 | **0.100** |
| 4 GB | global LRU | 255.5 | 1260.8 | 256.7 | 36.20% | 95.01% | 1633.1 ± 28.6 | 8406 | 0.119 |
| 4 GB | **core pinned** | 244.0 | 1223.5 | 246.1 | 36.20% | **97.30%** | 1596.5 ± 14.0 | 8076 | **0.124** |

At 2 GB: **2.13× fewer regions fetched** (818.9 → 384.0), 1.86× fewer bytes, core hit restored to
the level it has at 4 GB and 8 GB (97.30%), and **+18% throughput**. At 4 GB the same change buys
4.5% fewer regions and +4%: there, core was mostly surviving already.

## What was predicted, and what was wrong

Written before the run: regions 819 → ~380, core hit ~100%, expert hit ~15–20%. The first two were
right (384.0, 97.30%). **The expert prediction was wrong**: expert hit stayed at 4.68%, unchanged.
0.42 GB holds ~84 of 6,144 expert regions, but a single token needs 384 of them, so the evictable
window is smaller than one token's demand and reuse stays ~0 no matter how the budget is spent
between core and experts. Pinning fixes the tier that can be fixed; it cannot manufacture expert
reuse below one token's working set.

## What it does and does not mean

- **It is a same-budget policy win**, which is the only fair policy comparison. Against the *unpaged
  control* the pinned 2 GB run still loses (0.100 vs 0.176 tok/s): a 2 GB cap is 6× less memory than
  the OS gets for free, and no policy recovers that.
- **Above 4 GB it is a no-op**: at 8 GB and 11 GB core was never evicted (97.30% the whole time), so
  pinning changes nothing there. That is why the recommendation is **enable at ≤4 GB, harmless* above.
- **The trigger condition is now measured, not guessed**: pin when the budget is within ~2× of the
  core footprint (1.60 GB), which is exactly where global LRU starts evicting the hot tier.

## Recommendation

Ship `CP_PIN_CORE=1` as the recommended setting for budgets at or below ~4 GB, and leave the default
alone for now — a pinning default that silently runs over budget below the core footprint is a
deployment decision, not a measurement one, and the pager's contract is that the budget is a cap.
The report's pinned/evictable line makes the trade visible when it is enabled.

## Limits

One model, one disk, one prompt, 32 tokens; two reps on the deterministic counters (which are
identical) and the usual ±12% on physical bytes and ±4% on ms. The 2 GB regime is the one that
matters here and it is a regime a real deployment would avoid — the value of the result is that it
identifies *which* tier to protect when a budget is tight, and it is the first lever in this phase
that changed the read count materially without trading bytes for reads.
